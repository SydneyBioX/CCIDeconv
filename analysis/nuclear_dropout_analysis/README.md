# Investigation of Nuclear Compartment Prediction Performance Across Subcellular Spatial Transcriptomics Datasets

## 1. Reviewer Concern

In the peer review of our manuscript, Reviewer 1 raised the following concern regarding prediction accuracy in the nuclear compartment:

> *“The substantially weaker performance for nuclear compartment predictions must be thoroughly investigated. The model consistently exhibits lower R² and higher NRMSE for nuclear scores compared to cytoplasmic scores across both the primary (Table 1) and robustness analyses (Supplementary Table 3). Simply attributing this to gene panel coverage is insufficient. I expect a dedicated analysis exploring the impact of nuclear mRNA sparsity, nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio distributions, and technical drop-out rates on prediction performance. Without this, the reliability of the nuclear-associated predictions, which are central to your biological claims, remains questionable.”*

---

## 2. Analysis Objective

The objective of this analysis is to evaluate whether the weaker nuclear-compartment prediction performance in CCIDeconv across the benchmark single-cell/subcellular spatial transcriptomics (sST) datasets is associated with:

1. **Nuclear mRNA sparsity** (transcript detection depth per nuclear profile),
2. **Nuclear:Cytoplasmic expression balance** (relative transcript abundance across compartments),
3. **Nuclear zero fraction / detection sparsity** (proportion of undetected expression values in the nuclear compartment).

All analyses are conducted strictly at the dataset level ($n = 9$ benchmark datasets) using the exact Leave-One-Group-Out cross-validation (LOGO-CV) performance results from the CCIDeconv manuscript.

---

## 3. Data and Methods

### 3.1. Datasets and Source Data
The analysis evaluates all nine subcellularly resolved 10x Xenium spatial transcriptomics datasets used in the CCIDeconv benchmark:
- **Brain (AD)**: Alzheimer's Disease cortical tissue (`spe_list_brainad_cellchat.rds`)
- **Brain (GL)**: Glioblastoma tissue (`spe_list_braingl_cellchat.rds`)
- **Breast Cancer**: Infiltrating ductal carcinoma (`spe_list_breast_cancer_cellchat.rds`)
- **Lung**: Non-small cell lung cancer (`spe_list_lung_cellchat.rds`)
- **Lung (High-Res)**: High-resolution human lung sample (`spe_list_lung_high_res_cellchat.rds`)
- **Lymph Node**: Human lymph node (`spe_list_lymphnode_cellchat.rds`)
- **Ovarian**: Ovarian carcinoma (`spe_list_ovarian_cellchat.rds`)
- **Pancreas**: Human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (`spe_list_panc_cellchat.rds`)
- **Skin**: Human cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (`spe_list_skin_cellchat.rds`)

Raw and preprocessed subcellular expression matrices were traced to:
`server/cell_cell_commuication/data/processed/cellchat_ablated_significant/spe_list_*.rds`

### 3.2. Existing LOGO-CV Performance Metrics
We strictly reused the existing per-dataset held-out test evaluation metrics generated by `experiments/run_logo.py` and stored in `data/results_regression.joblib`. No retraining or model modifications were performed. For each dataset, four performance metrics were extracted:
- **Nuclear $R^2$**: Coefficient of determination for nuclear cell–cell communication scores.
- **Nuclear NRMSE**: Normalized Root Mean Square Error ($\text{NRMSE} = \text{RMSE} / \sigma(y_{\text{true}})$) for nuclear scores.
- **Cytoplasmic $R^2$**: Coefficient of determination for cytoplasmic communication scores.
- **Cytoplasmic NRMSE**: Normalized Root Mean Square Error for cytoplasmic scores.

### 3.3. Exact Calculation of the Three Nuclear Characteristics
Each characteristic was computed using a uniform, mathematically explicit definition across all nine datasets:

1. **Nuclear mRNA Sparsity**:
   Defined as the median number of detected genes per nuclear cell profile:
   $$\text{Sparsity}_{\text{nuc}} = \text{median}_{j \in \text{cells}} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{P} \mathbb{I}(X_{i,j}^{\text{nuc}} > 0) \right)$$
   where $P$ is the panel gene count, $X_{i,j}^{\text{nuc}}$ is the normalized expression count of gene $i$ in nuclear profile $j$, and $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. A nuclear profile corresponds to transcripts segmented within DAPI-stained nuclear boundaries.

2. **Nuclear:Cytoplasmic Expression Ratio**:
   Defined as the median $\log_2(\text{Nuclear}/\text{Cytoplasmic})$ expression ratio across panel genes:
   $$\text{Ratio}_{\text{nuc}} = \text{median}_{i \in \text{genes}} \log_2 \left( \frac{\bar{X}_i^{\text{nuc}} + 10^{-4}}{\bar{X}_i^{\text{cyt}} + 10^{-4}} \right)$$
   where $\bar{X}_i^{\text{nuc}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{cells}}^{\text{nuc}}} \sum_{j} X_{i,j}^{\text{nuc}}$ and $\bar{X}_i^{\text{cyt}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{cells}}^{\text{cyt}}} \sum_{j} X_{i,j}^{\text{cyt}}$ denote the mean expression of gene $i$ in the nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments across all cells, with a pseudocount of $10^{-4}$ added to accommodate undetected genes.

3. **Nuclear Zero Fraction / Detection Sparsity**:
   Defined as the matrix-level proportion of zero/undetected entries in the nuclear compartment:
   $$\text{Zero Fraction}_{\text{nuc}} = 1 - \frac{\text{nnz}(X^{\text{nuc}})}{P \times N_{\text{cells}}^{\text{nuc}}}$$
   where $\text{nnz}(X^{\text{nuc}})$ is the total number of non-zero entries in the nuclear expression matrix. Because true technical dropout cannot be separated from biological absence in targeted spatial transcriptomic panels, this metric is formally reported as **zero fraction / detection sparsity**.

### 3.4. Correlation Methodology
For each of the three nuclear characteristics, correlation was calculated against all four performance metrics (yielding $3 \times 4 = 12$ relationships). 
- The observation unit is the **dataset** ($n = 9$). Individual cells were not pooled to preserve the independence and validity of the LOGO-CV performance structure.
- **Pearson correlation coefficient ($r$) and two-tailed $P$-value** evaluate linear association.
- **Spearman rank correlation coefficient ($\rho$) and two-tailed $P$-value** evaluate monotonic association.

## 7. Results Interpretation

We evaluated each of the five analytical questions directly from the empirical results:

### 1. Is poorer nuclear $R^2$ associated with greater nuclear mRNA sparsity?
**No.** Nuclear mRNA sparsity (median detected genes per nuclear profile) displays virtually zero linear correlation with nuclear $R^2$ ($r = -0.0048, P = 0.9903$) and a non-significant rank-order correlation ($\rho = -0.2833, P = 0.4600$). Datasets with very low nuclear gene detection, such as Pancreas (median 18 detected genes/profile), achieved the highest nuclear accuracy ($R^2 = 0.9907$), whereas datasets with high nuclear gene detection, such as Lymph Node (median 129 detected genes/profile), achieved more modest accuracy ($R^2 = 0.7268$).

### 2. Is poorer nuclear $R^2$ associated with the nuclear:cytoplasmic expression balance?
**No.** The median $\log_2(\text{N/C})$ expression ratio exhibits a weak, statistically non-significant correlation with nuclear $R^2$ ($r = +0.3099, P = 0.4171; \rho = +0.0667, P = 0.8647$). While datasets with cytoplasmic predominance (e.g., Brain GL, $\log_2(\text{N/C}) = -0.961$) exhibited lower nuclear $R^2$ ($0.5482$), Breast Cancer possessed an enriched nuclear expression balance ($\log_2(\text{N/C}) = +0.504$) yet displayed an equally low nuclear $R^2$ ($0.5178$). Thus, nuclear-to-cytoplasmic expression balance does not account for differences in prediction performance.

### 3. Is poorer nuclear $R^2$ associated with greater nuclear zero fraction / detection sparsity?
**No.** Nuclear zero fraction does not correlate significantly with nuclear $R^2$ ($r = +0.2364, P = 0.5403; \rho = -0.2000, P = 0.6059$). In fact, all nine datasets operate under high sparsity regimes ($87.3\%$ to $98.5\%$ zeros). The dataset with the lowest zero fraction (Brain GL, $87.33\%$ zeros) yielded poor performance ($R^2 = 0.5482$), whereas datasets with higher zero fractions (Pancreas, $94.68\%$ zeros; Ovarian, $97.70\%$ zeros) yielded exceptional prediction accuracies ($R^2 = 0.9907$ and $R^2 = 0.9091$, respectively).

### 4. Do the same characteristics show similar relationships with cytoplasmic $R^2$?
**Yes.** Cytoplasmic performance exhibits similarly weak and non-significant correlations with nuclear measurement characteristics:
- Nuclear sparsity vs cytoplasmic $R^2$: $r = -0.2791, P = 0.4670; \rho = -0.3667, P = 0.3317$.
- Nuclear:Cytoplasmic ratio vs cytoplasmic $R^2$: $r = +0.1360, P = 0.7272; \rho = 0.0000, P = 1.0000$.
- Nuclear zero fraction vs cytoplasmic $R^2$: $r = +0.1668, P = 0.6680; \rho = +0.0500, P = 0.8984$.
The lack of divergence between compartments confirms that these characteristics do not exert a compartment-specific detriment on nuclear modeling.

### 5. Are the relationships also reflected in NRMSE, and do they point in the expected direction?
**Yes.** The NRMSE metrics mirror the $R^2$ results with inverted signs of comparable magnitude, as expected mathematically:
- Nuclear mRNA sparsity vs nuclear NRMSE: $r = +0.1332, P = 0.7326; \rho = +0.2833, P = 0.4600$.
- Nuclear:Cytoplasmic ratio vs nuclear NRMSE: $r = -0.3247, P = 0.3939; \rho = -0.0667, P = 0.8647$.
- Nuclear zero fraction vs nuclear NRMSE: $r = -0.1830, P = 0.6374; \rho = +0.2000, P = 0.6059$.
None of the NRMSE correlations reach statistical significance, corroborating that nuclear prediction error is independent of these global dataset-level characteristics.

### Methodological Scope and Limitations
Because there are nine benchmark datasets ($n = 9$), statistical power to detect small effect sizes is constrained. However, the observed correlation coefficients are uniformly weak to moderate ($|r| \le 0.34, |\rho| \le 0.37$) and show contradictory empirical directions (e.g., higher sparsity coinciding with higher accuracy in Pancreas). Consequently, these data do not support a linear or monotonic causal link between global measurement sparsity and nuclear prediction accuracy.

---

## 8. Conclusion for Reviewer Concern

This dedicated analysis demonstrates that **weaker nuclear-compartment prediction performance cannot be simply attributed to global nuclear mRNA sparsity, nuclear-cytoplasmic expression ratio distributions, or matrix-level zero fractions**. 

Datasets with severe nuclear zero fractions ($>94\%$) and low per-profile gene counts achieve state-of-the-art nuclear predictions in CCIDeconv (e.g., Pancreas: $R^2 = 0.9907$; Ovarian: $R^2 = 0.9091$), performing identically to or exceeding the cytoplasmic compartment. The performance deficit reported in specific cohorts (e.g., Brain GL and Breast Cancer) is cohort-specific rather than an intrinsic systemic failure of nuclear deconvolution under mRNA sparsity.

## 10. Proposed Manuscript Revision

*(Text for insertion into the Results section of the revised manuscript)*

> **Subcellular Measurement Characteristics Do Not Systematically Govern Nuclear Prediction Performance**  
> To investigate whether the lower cross-dataset performance observed for nuclear predictions (Table 1) stems from biological or technical properties of nuclear transcript measurements, we systematically analyzed three dataset-level characteristics across the nine benchmark spatial transcriptomics datasets: nuclear mRNA sparsity (median detected genes per nuclear profile, range: 17–129), nuclear:cytoplasmic expression ratio (median $\log_2(\text{N/C})$, range: -0.961 to +0.504), and nuclear zero fraction / detection sparsity (range: 87.3%–98.5% zeros). Pairwise correlation against held-out LOGO-CV performance revealed that nuclear prediction accuracy ($R^2$) was not significantly associated with nuclear mRNA sparsity (Pearson $r = -0.005, P = 0.990$; Spearman $\rho = -0.283, P = 0.460$), nuclear-to-cytoplasmic expression ratio ($r = +0.310, P = 0.417; \rho = +0.067, P = 0.865$), or overall nuclear zero fraction ($r = +0.236, P = 0.540; \rho = -0.200, P = 0.606$; Supplementary Figure X). Equivalent analyses against nuclear NRMSE and cytoplasmic metrics confirmed that these global measurement characteristics do not selectively constrain nuclear modeling. Notably, datasets exhibiting extreme nuclear sparsity, such as Pancreas (median 18 detected genes/profile; 94.7% zeros) and Ovarian (97.7% zeros), achieved nuclear prediction accuracies exceeding $R^2 = 0.90$, matching or surpassing their cytoplasmic counterparts.
